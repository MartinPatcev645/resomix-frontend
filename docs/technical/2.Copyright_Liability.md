2. Regulatory Compliance: Copyright, Liability, and Article 17
Operating within the European Union mandates strict adherence to the Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market. Article 17 of this directive has fundamentally shifted the liability landscape for platforms that host user-uploaded content or aggregate copyrighted material. For Resomix, which stores and organizes music for professional use, this is the single most significant legal and technical risk vector.

2.1 The OCSSP Classification and "Best Efforts"
Under Article 17, Resomix would likely be classified as an Online Content-Sharing Service Provider (OCSSP). This classification removes the "safe harbor" protections that previously existed under the e-Commerce Directive. An OCSSP is liable for copyright infringement the moment a file is uploaded, unless it can demonstrate that it made "best efforts" to obtain authorization and, crucially, "best efforts" to prevent the availability of specific unauthorized works.

"Best efforts" is a legal term with a technical solution: Automated Content Recognition (ACR). Rights holders will not sign a distribution deal without a guarantee that the platform can filter out unauthorized uploads or "leaked" tracks before they become publicly accessible. The expectation is a "stay-down" mechanism—once a rights holder flags a track, it must stay off the platform permanently.

2.2 Integrating Automated Content Recognition (ACR)
Building a proprietary ACR system is technically demanding and legally risky; if the proprietary system fails, the liability rests entirely on Resomix. The industry standard is to license this capability from established vendors who maintain global databases of audio fingerprints.

2.2.1 Vendor Selection: Audible Magic vs. ACRCloud
Audible Magic is the incumbent leader in this space, trusted by major platforms like SoundCloud, Facebook, and Twitch. Their technology is embedded deeply into the major labels' workflows. When a label delivers content to Audible Magic, it is fingerprinted and added to a global blocklist/allowlist registry. Using Audible Magic's SDK or API allows Resomix to check every ingested file against this registry. If a match is found, the API returns business rules (e.g., "Block," "Monetize," "Allow"). Integrating Audible Magic is often viewed as the "gold standard" of compliance by major labels.
ACRCloud offers a competitive alternative, often with more flexible pricing for startups and superior performance in identifying cover songs and remixes—a critical feature for a DJ-centric platform. Given that DJs often upload mashups or edits, the ability to identify the underlying composition within a remix is vital for proper royalty attribution. ACRCloud’s fingerprinting technology is robust against pitch shifts and speed changes, common techniques in DJ performances.

2.2.2 The Ingestion-Time Scanning Workflow
The technical implementation of this safeguard must occur synchronously during ingestion. When a file lands in the MinIO bucket, the ingestion worker must generate a fingerprint of the audio. This fingerprint is sent to the ACR provider's API. Only upon receiving a "clean" status code should the file be moved to the permanent storage array and indexed by the R-BeatNet engine. Files that match a "Block" rule must be quarantined, and a notification generated for the uploader. This pre-publication filtering workflow is the primary technical answer to rights holders' concerns regarding piracy.

2.3 GDPR and Transatlantic Data Flows
European labels will scrutinize the platform's handling of personal data. While music files themselves are not personal data, the metadata associated with them—specifically identifiers linked to artists and songwriters—can be. Furthermore, user data (DJ profiles, listening history) is strictly protected under GDPR.
A critical compliance gap often arises in the use of US-based infrastructure. If Resomix uses US-based services for any part of its stack (e.g., Cloudflare for CDN, external APIs for enrichment), it must rely on Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) to legitimize the transfer of data outside the EU/EEA. Since the Schrems II ruling invalidated the Privacy Shield, relying solely on US hosting without supplementary measures is a compliance violation. Given that the storage and compute are on Hetzner (a German provider) and Alwyzon (Austrian), the core data residency compliance is strong. However, any backup strategy involving US providers (like Backblaze) must be configured with encryption keys managed exclusively in Europe, ensuring that the cloud provider cannot access the plaintext data, thus satisfying the "supplementary measures" requirement of the EDPB.
